# UGO-Omics reveals a recurrent regeneration–resilience toolkit across underground-organ systems

## Abstract

Underground organs such as rhizomes, tubers, storage roots, corms, and related structures underlie perenniality, vegetative propagation, carbohydrate storage, stress survival, and post-disturbance regrowth in many plant lineages. Despite their biological and agricultural importance, underground-organ research remains fragmented across species, organs, and omics datasets. Here, we present UGO-Omics, a cross-species, evolution-informed discovery platform for underground-organ biology. UGO-Omics integrates a 51-species underground-organ atlas with an 11-species high-confidence analytical backbone, orthogroup-based candidate transfer, researcher-facing query tools, and an independent BUSCO-based phylogenetic framework. Using this framework, we identify recurrent candidate modules associated with meristem/regrowth, stress resilience, hormone-related regulation, developmental transcriptional control, and cell-wall remodeling. A known-category recovery benchmark shows that the current module layer recovers expected biological categories linked to underground-organ development and persistence. Integrating module recurrence, phylogenetic dispersion, benchmark recovery, and species-level support reveals a candidate regeneration–resilience core toolkit that recurs across phylogenetically divergent underground-organ systems. We propose that morphologically distinct underground organs may repeatedly recruit conserved developmental, survival, regulatory, and structural-remodeling modules through module-level convergence and regulatory redeployment. UGO-Omics provides a reusable platform and evolutionary framework for prioritizing candidate genes and modules underlying underground-organ diversification, persistence, and regeneration.

## Significance

Underground organs are central to plant survival, regeneration, vegetative propagation, and crop productivity, but their study has lacked a unified cross-species omics framework. UGO-Omics addresses this gap by integrating a broad 51-species atlas, an 11-species high-confidence analytical backbone, phylogenetic context, query tools, benchmark recovery, and biological discovery modules. The platform suggests that diverse underground organs may repeatedly recruit a conserved regeneration–resilience toolkit, providing a new framework for studying underground-organ evolution and function.

## Introduction

Underground organs are among the most important but least integrated systems in plant biology. Structures such as rhizomes, tubers, storage roots, corms, and related underground organs contribute to perenniality, vegetative propagation, storage, dormancy, stress survival, and post-disturbance regeneration. These traits are central to plant adaptation and crop productivity, yet underground-organ research remains fragmented across species and organ types.

A major challenge is that underground organs are not a single equivalent structure. Rhizomes, tubers, storage roots, and corm-like structures can arise from different developmental contexts. This makes strict organ-level homology difficult to assume. However, these structures share functional demands: they must support persistence, resource allocation, developmental-state transitions, tissue remodeling, and regrowth. This creates an opportunity to study underground-organ biology at the level of conserved functional modules rather than relying only on organ morphology.

Here we present UGO-Omics, a cross-species, evolution-informed discovery platform for underground-organ biology. UGO-Omics combines a broad 51-species underground-organ atlas, an 11-species high-confidence analytical backbone, orthogroup-based candidate transfer, researcher-facing query workflows, an independent BUSCO-based phylogenetic framework, known-category recovery benchmark, and a biological discovery case. Using this framework, we identify a candidate regeneration–resilience core toolkit that recurs across phylogenetically divergent underground-organ systems.

## Results

### Construction of UGO-Omics as a cross-species underground-organ platform

UGO-Omics was designed to address the lack of a unified comparative framework for underground-organ biology. The platform integrates data across species, organs, orthogroups, candidate modules, and user-facing discovery tools. A broad 51-species atlas provides taxonomic and organ-level breadth, while an 11-species high-confidence backbone supports more detailed comparative analysis and candidate transfer.

This tiered design separates breadth from analytical depth. The 51-species atlas captures the wider landscape of underground-organ diversity, whereas the 11-species backbone provides the core framework for orthogroup mapping, module scoring, phylogenetic interpretation, and discovery workflows.

### A high-confidence 11-species backbone enables orthogroup-based candidate transfer

The 11-species backbone was selected to represent major underground-organ systems and phylogenetic breadth while retaining sufficient data quality for cross-species candidate transfer. Orthogroup-based mapping allows UGO-Omics to connect candidate genes and modules across divergent species, enabling users to move from a gene, sequence, species, or organ question to biologically interpretable candidate information.

### An independent BUSCO phylogeny provides evolutionary context for the UGO backbone

To place the 11-species backbone into an independent evolutionary context, we reconstructed a BUSCO-based phylogenetic framework. The main tree was built from a relaxed 9-of-11 BUSCO matrix containing 531 loci and 279,498 amino-acid sites. A stricter 10-of-11 BUSCO sensitivity tree provided an additional topology check. This tree was used as a phylogenetic context for interpreting module recurrence, rather than as a definitive reconstruction of underground-organ origins.

### Researcher-facing workflows enable sequence-, gene-list-, species-, and organ-based discovery

UGO-Omics was built as a practical discovery platform rather than a static database. The current interface includes Sequence-to-UGO, Gene-list-to-UGO, Resource Finder, Organ Kits, Demo Results, Use Cases, and Quick Guide sections. These workflows support common entry points for underground-organ researchers: a protein sequence, a candidate gene list, a target species, an organ type, or a need for downloadable resources.

### Conserved candidate modules recur across phylogenetically divergent underground-organ systems

Mapping candidate modules onto the BUSCO phylogenetic context revealed recurrent species-level support for modules associated with meristem/regrowth, stress resilience, hormone-related regulation, developmental transcriptional control, and cell-wall remodeling. Four modules were detected across all 11 backbone species and spanned both monocot and eudicot representatives. Cell-wall remodeling showed a more restricted but still cross-lineage pattern.

These patterns suggest that underground-organ systems may share conserved module-level features even when the organs themselves are morphologically and developmentally non-equivalent.

### UGO-Omics supports a regeneration–resilience toolkit model

We developed a regeneration–resilience toolkit model to interpret module recurrence. In this model, underground-organ diversification may involve recurrent recruitment of modules associated with regeneration capacity, stress survival, developmental-state switching, regulatory network control, and structural remodeling. This framework emphasizes module-level convergence and regulatory redeployment rather than strict organ-level homology.

### Known-category recovery benchmark supports module-level candidate prioritization

To evaluate whether UGO-Omics recovers expected biological categories, we constructed a known-category recovery benchmark. The benchmark included meristem/bud regrowth, hormone-mediated developmental-state switching, stress survival, transcriptional network control, structural remodeling, storage metabolism, and organ identity or patterning categories. The current five-module mechanism layer recovered nine of ten benchmark items overall and all nine current-scope benchmark items. Storage metabolism was retained as a future expansion target.

### A high-impact discovery case identifies a recurrent regeneration–resilience core toolkit

Integrating module recurrence, phylogenetic dispersion, benchmark recovery, and species-level support identified a recurrent regeneration–resilience core toolkit. The highest-scoring core modules were meristem/regrowth, stress resilience, hormone-related regulation, and developmental transcription-factor regulation. Each was detected across all 11 backbone species, spanned monocot and eudicot representatives, and recovered expected benchmark categories. Cell-wall remodeling formed a supporting structural component.

Together, these results support a biological discovery case in which diverse underground organs may repeatedly recruit a conserved regeneration–resilience core through module-level convergence and regulatory redeployment.

## Discussion

UGO-Omics fills a resource and conceptual gap in underground-organ biology by connecting a broad species atlas, a high-confidence analytical backbone, phylogenetic context, candidate modules, benchmark recovery, and researcher-facing workflows. The platform is designed to be both reusable and biologically interpretable.

A key outcome of UGO-Omics is the regeneration–resilience toolkit model. Underground organs are morphologically diverse and should not be treated as a single homologous structure. However, they share functional demands for persistence, regrowth, developmental-state switching, and tissue remodeling. UGO-Omics suggests that these shared demands may be met through recurrent recruitment of conserved modules.

The BUSCO-based tree provides an independent phylogenetic context for interpreting these patterns. The recurrence of candidate modules across monocot and eudicot representatives supports a module-level convergence model. This does not establish functional validation of individual genes, nor does it reconstruct the definitive evolutionary origin of underground organs. Instead, it provides a prioritized, testable framework for future functional and evolutionary studies.

The known-category recovery benchmark supports the credibility of the candidate-prioritization logic. UGO-Omics recovers expected categories related to meristem activity, stress survival, hormone switching, transcriptional regulation, and structural remodeling. Storage metabolism remains an important future expansion target.

UGO-Omics also provides practical value through Sequence-to-UGO, Gene-list-to-UGO, Resource Finder, Organ Kits, Demo Results, and Use Cases. These tools allow users to enter the platform from sequences, gene lists, species, organs, or resource needs.

Future work should expand the platform with additional storage-metabolism modules, stronger organ-level evidence curation, QTL/GWAS integration, single-cell and spatial layers, and experimental testing of high-priority candidate modules.

## Methods

### Species atlas and high-confidence backbone

UGO-Omics was organized as a tiered framework. A broad 51-species atlas provides species and organ breadth, while an 11-species high-confidence backbone provides analytical depth for orthogroup-based candidate transfer, phylogenetic interpretation, and module recurrence analysis.

### BUSCO-based phylogenetic context

A BUSCO-based species tree was reconstructed using protein sequences from the 11-species backbone. BUSCO analyses were performed using the embryophyta_odb10 lineage dataset. Because strict 11-species single-copy overlap was limited by duplicated BUSCO recovery in several proteomes, the main matrix used BUSCO loci recovered as single-copy in at least 9 of the 11 species. Missing species for individual loci were coded as gaps. The resulting matrix contained 531 BUSCO loci and 279,498 amino-acid sites. IQ-TREE was used for maximum-likelihood inference with model selection and branch support estimation. A stricter 10-of-11 BUSCO matrix was used as a sensitivity analysis.

### Module phylogenetic distribution

Five prioritized candidate modules were mapped onto the 11-species BUSCO phylogenetic context: meristem/regrowth, stress resilience, hormone-related regulation, developmental transcription-factor regulation, and cell-wall remodeling. Species-level module occurrence was derived from the UGO candidate layer and interpreted as module-level recurrence. Underground-organ categories were treated as species-associated annotations.

### Evolutionary mechanism layer

Each module was assigned to a mechanistic component: regeneration capacity, stress survival, developmental-state switching, regulatory network control, or structural remodeling. A module recurrence index summarized species breadth, monocot/eudicot dispersion, and diversity of species-associated underground-organ contexts. These scores were used to support the regeneration–resilience toolkit model.

### Known-category recovery benchmark

A known-category benchmark panel was constructed to test whether the UGO mechanism layer recovered expected biological categories related to underground-organ development and persistence. Recovery was assessed at the category/module level and was not interpreted as functional validation of individual genes.

### Biological discovery case

The high-impact discovery case integrated module recurrence, phylogenetic dispersion, benchmark recovery, and species-level support. Modules with the strongest combined evidence were classified as core discovery modules. This analysis was used to prioritize a candidate regeneration–resilience core toolkit for future testing.

## Data and resource availability

The UGO-Omics web interface, downloadable resource tables, source JSON files, toolkits, example inputs, and example outputs are provided through the public repository and release package. Large sequence databases are distributed separately when needed to avoid repository size limitations.

## Limitations

UGO-Omics provides a hypothesis-generating framework. The current version does not experimentally test individual candidate genes, does not infer definitive underground-organ origins, and does not treat diverse underground organs as a single homologous structure. Future versions should expand storage metabolism, organ-level evidence curation, QTL/GWAS integration, and functional testing.
